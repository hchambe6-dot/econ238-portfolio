Are EVs actually cleaner than gas cars?

You’ve heard the objections: “The battery is dirty to make,” “It just runs on coal,” “They’re heavier, so the tires pollute more.” Each one is partly true. Here is how big each effect really is, on one chart.

Hover or tap a dot for the break-even details.

True: EVs start behind

Making a 75 kWh battery adds about 5.6 t of CO₂, so a new EV is roughly 80% “dirtier” than a new gas car on day one.

They catch up fast

On the U.S.-average grid the EV is ahead after ~22k miles (~1.6 years). Over 200,000 miles it emits 56% less CO₂, and 65% less on New York’s grid.

Coal shrinks the win

Even on West Virginia’s grid, the dirtiest in the U.S. (mostly coal), the EV still comes out 16% ahead. It just takes ~60k miles to get there.

What about the tires?

The professor’s point is real. EVs are about 300–700 lb heavier, and more weight means more tire and road wear. Tire particles are now a bigger source of fine-particle pollution than exhaust from modern gas cars. But EVs also make almost no brake dust (regenerative braking does most of the stopping) and have no tailpipe. The OECD added all of it up:

Verdict: for particle pollution it’s close to a tie. A big long-range EV is a few percent worse, a small EV is ~12% better. For CO₂ it isn’t close: 55–70% less on most U.S. grids. The weight problem is a reason to want smaller EVs (and better tires), not to keep driving gas cars.

How this was built (so you can rerun it)

  1. Gas car: 30 mpg midsize sedan. Burning a gallon of gasoline releases 8.89 kg CO₂ (EPA); +24% for drilling, refining and shipping the fuel gives ≈ 367 g CO₂/mile.
  2. EV: 0.30 kWh/mile from the wall (charging losses included), +5% power-line losses, times each state’s grid CO₂ rate from the EIA State Electricity Profiles (2024).
  3. Building the cars: 7.0 t CO₂e for the car itself (either type). The EV adds a 75 kWh (~300-mile) battery at 75 kg CO₂e per kWh, for +5.6 t. Published estimates range from ~60 to 100 kg/kWh (Argonne GREET, IEA Global EV Outlook).
  4. Break-even = extra build emissions ÷ CO₂ saved per mile. Lifetime = build + 200,000 miles of driving.
  5. Particles: the OECD’s 2020 report Non-exhaust Particulate Emissions from Road Transport estimates EV vs. gas PM2.5 for light (~100-mile) and heavy (~300-mile) EVs, counting exhaust, tire, brake and road wear.

What this leaves out

  • Grids are frozen at 2024 levels. They are getting cleaner every year, so this understates the EV advantage over a 15-year car life.
  • The grid rates are for power generated in each state. Power trades across state lines, so the true mix behind your charger can differ.
  • It doesn’t cover mining impacts beyond CO₂ (water, land, labor in lithium, cobalt and nickel supply chains), battery recycling, or a mid-life battery replacement.
  • Bigger EVs (trucks, SUVs) have larger batteries and longer break-evens, but the comparison gas trucks burn more fuel too.

Ask the next question

  • Would a hybrid (about 50 mpg, small battery) beat an EV on West Virginia’s grid?
  • What is the break-even for an electric F-150 vs. a gas F-150?
  • If the U.S. grid keeps cleaning up at the 2015–2024 pace, how much bigger does the EV advantage get?
