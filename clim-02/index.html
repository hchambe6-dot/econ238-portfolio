Who actually moves global temperature?

Our class example asked what happens if only the U.S. went net-zero: about 0.14–0.17°C less warming by 2100. Here is the same experiment for China, India, the EU and groups of them, all on one °C scale.

Hover or tap a bar for the numbers behind it. Full table below.

China > U.S. + EU

China going net-zero alone avoids 0.28–0.49°C, more than the U.S. and EU together (0.17–0.23°C).

India is the rising lever

India emits less than the U.S. today, but its emissions are still growing. Over the century its net-zero is worth 0.19–0.29°C, more than the U.S. in both scenarios.

The big four aren’t enough

Even if all four (≈59% of today’s CO₂) went net-zero, warming would still reach about 2.0°C (Medium) or 2.4°C (High). The rest of the world avoids slightly more than all four combined.

What this shows

A country’s effect on 2100 temperature depends on how much CO₂ it will emit from now on, not on how much it emits today or has emitted in the past. Warming tracks total cumulative CO₂ almost linearly, so “who moves the needle” comes down to whose future emissions are biggest. The EU is a large economy, but its emissions are already small and falling, so it has little left to cut. India is the reverse: modest today but growing, so its future pile is large. China is huge on both counts.

Same scale, different story: a rich-country-only climate deal (U.S. + EU) buys roughly the same warming reduction as India alone, and less than China alone. Whatever happens in Washington or Brussels, the 2100 thermometer is mostly set in Asia and in the “rest of world”: Russia, Japan, Southeast Asia, the Middle East, Africa and Latin America.

How this was built (so you can rerun it)

  1. Starting point: 2024 fossil-fuel + cement CO₂ for each region from the Global Carbon Project, via Our World in Data.
  2. Two futures to 2100, styled on the CMIP7 ScenarioMIP Medium (current policy) and High (worst case / policy rollback) scenarios. Each region gets simple growth rates:
    RegionMedium pathHigh path
    U.S.−1%/yrflat
    EU−2.5%/yr to 2050, then −2%−1%/yr
    Chinaflat to 2030, −1.5%/yr to 2060, then −2%+1%/yr to 2040, then flat
    India+3%/yr to 2040, +1% to 2060, then −1%+3.5%/yr to 2050, then +1%
    Rest of world+1%/yr to 2050, flat to 2070, then −1%+2%/yr to 2060, then +1%
    Global totals: Medium ≈ 40 Gt in 2050 → 26 Gt in 2100; High ≈ 55 Gt → 81 Gt.
  3. The counterfactual: the chosen country or group goes to net-zero CO₂ from 2025, and everyone else stays on the scenario path (the same thought experiment as the class example).
  4. CO₂ → temperature: avoided cumulative CO₂ × IPCC AR6 TCRE (0.45°C per 1,000 GtCO₂; likely 0.27–0.63, shown as whiskers). A small non-CO₂ factor (×1.08 Medium, ×1.01 High) makes the global paths land on the class example’s end points: 2.65°C and 3.45°C in 2100, from 1.33°C in 2025.
  5. Check: this method gives the U.S. 0.13°C / 0.17°C, against the class example’s 0.14 / 0.17°C. Close enough to put every bar on the same scale as that exhibit.

What this leaves out

  • It isn’t an official CMIP7 model run. The regional paths are stylized assumptions, and changing them changes the bars.
  • Only fossil and cement CO₂ are counted directly. Land-use CO₂ and methane are handled only through the small calibration factor.
  • “Everyone else unchanged” ignores spillovers: cheaper clean tech spreading abroad, or carbon leakage to other countries.
  • Instant net-zero is an upper bound. A realistic phase-out by 2050 cuts each number by roughly 10–25% (see table).

Ask the next question

  • Per person, who would be giving up the most? (India’s per-capita CO₂ is about ⅙ of the U.S. level.)
  • Which part of the “rest of world” (Southeast Asia, Africa, the Middle East) is the next China?
  • What if clean-tech spillovers mean that one region’s net-zero drags others 20% lower too?
