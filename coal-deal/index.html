Proposition 238: Pay Once to Retire Michigan's Campbell Coal Plant

CAST YOUR VOTE: YES · NO · YES, IF…

Anonymous. Takes one minute. Read below first if you want the full argument.

The Ballot Question

Shall Michigan issue up to $900 million in low-interest, ratepayer-backed bonds to buy the remaining value of Consumers Energy's J.H. Campbell coal plant in West Olive; permanently retire all three units within 90 days of purchase, or as soon as no federal order requires them to run; record a deed restriction banning coal burning on the site; and set aside at least $100 million for the plant's workers and local governments?

A YES vote means: the state buys out the plant, it stops burning coal, and the site can never burn coal again. You can check whether this happened: the plant is either generating power or it isn't.

What's the Deal?

We are buying roughly 8 million tons of avoided CO₂ per year, plus cleaner air in West Michigan. We give up a 1,420-megawatt power plant built in 1962 that was already approved to retire in May 2025, until a federal emergency order kept it running at a cost of $295 million in its first 13 months. Lake Michigan communities and everyone downwind benefit. Plant workers and Ottawa County, which lose jobs and tax revenue, bear the costs. Electricity customers pay, through bonds at about 3–4% interest instead of the 6–8% utilities usually charge, so much of the cost refinances money customers already owe. The scale: $300–900 million up front, for 9 to 114 million tons of CO₂ avoided, depending on how long the plant would otherwise have run.

The Best Case for YES

This is one of the cheapest climate deals available. In a middle scenario, closing Campbell costs about $13 per ton of CO₂ avoided, well below most estimates of the damage each ton causes. That is before counting health benefits: one estimate cited by Michigan legislators puts the plant's health damages at $389–879 million every year. Keeping it open costs roughly $267 million a year; closing it is a one-time payment. The financing tool is not hypothetical. Missouri, Colorado, New Mexico and other states already use ratepayer-backed bonds to retire coal plants early, and Missouri used them to close the 200-megawatt Asbury plant about 15 years ahead of schedule. Michigan regulators had already approved Campbell's retirement as the cheaper option. This proposition makes that decision permanent and pays the workers and the town for what they lose, instead of leaving them to absorb the shock alone.

The Best Case for NO

We might pay for something that would happen anyway. A federal appeals court has already ruled the first emergency order unlawful. If the courts end the orders, the plant could close for free, and this deal would hand a utility hundreds of millions of dollars for nothing. If it doesn't close, a state ballot measure cannot override a federal order, so the plant keeps running and the bonds buy a promise, not a closure. Even a successful buyout may not cut emissions much: if natural gas plants fill the gap, the climate benefit falls by about 40%. Finally, paying owners to shut polluting plants creates a dangerous precedent. Every coal plant owner in America would learn that the way to get a check is to threaten to keep running. Electricity customers, including low-income households, would carry bond payments for decades.

Where Is the Body Buried?

The whole case rests on one number nobody knows: how many more years Campbell would run without this deal. If the answer is 14, the deal saves money and avoids over 100 million tons of CO₂. If it's two, we pay about $107 per ton, which is no bargain. That number depends on judges and federal officials, not on Michigan voters. And West Olive loses its largest employer and taxpayer. $100 million softens that, but it doesn't replace a working plant.

Why Isn't This Already Happening?

Because the people who decide are not the people who pay or benefit. The federal government controls whether the plant runs, Michigan controls the financing, and customers in 11 states are being billed for keeping it open, so no single decision-maker captures the full benefit of closing it. Utilities earn returns on plants they own, which gives them little reason to volunteer for a buyout. The costs are concentrated: a specific town, specific workers and a utility that can lobby hard. The benefits are spread across millions of people breathing slightly cleaner air and a climate that improves invisibly. And politically, "paying a polluter" sounds terrible even when it's cheap. Even where these bonds are legal, they are rarely used: across the ten states allowing them, only about 2.7 gigawatts of nearly 50 gigawatts of coal have been retired this way.
